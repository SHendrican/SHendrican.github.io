De-icer manufacturing — a new account in a corrosive, seasonal operation

The challenge

A de-icer manufacturer in Ontario — my first new account at BDI — runs manufacturing equipment in a corrosive environment, with seasonal demand and a need for reliable component availability. When production peaks, the equipment has to keep running, and the components behind it have to be there.

Case study · Bearing identification

Identifying a replacement bearing from dimensions alone

The challenge

A replacement bearing was needed with no usable part number to work from — just the bearing itself and its measurements: a 13 mm bore, 32 mm outside diameter, 10 mm wide (13×32×10).

Working it through

That 32×10 envelope is the 6201 family — but a standard 6201 has a 12 mm bore, not 13 mm. The extra millimetre on the bore makes it the non-standard variant, 6201-13. Quoting the standard number off the envelope alone would have put a bearing on the customer’s shaft that doesn’t fit it.

The takeaway

Dimensions identify the bearing — but every dimension has to be checked. The bore is the one that separates a standard catalogue number from the variant that actually fits.

Product reference · identification guides

Bearings

Ball and roller bearings — what they do

Support rotating shafts and carry radial and axial loads while keeping friction low. The main families are ball bearings, roller bearings (cylindrical, spherical and tapered), mounted units, precision machine-tool bearings, and linear guides and shafting.

Common applicationsMounted units on conveyors, precision bearings in machine tools, linear guides in automation, and general shaft support across plant equipment.

Questions to askWhat shaft does it sit on, and what housing does it fit? Sealed, shielded or open? What speed and load does it see — and how did the old one fail?

Bearings

Identifying a bearing from its dimensions

With no readable part number, measure the three dimensions — bore, outside diameter and width — then confirm the bearing type, whether it is sealed, shielded or open, and the internal clearance. The dimension set usually points to a bearing family, but every dimension still has to be checked individually.

Example: a bearing measuring 13 × 32 × 10 mm sits in the 6201 size envelope (32 mm outside diameter, 10 mm wide), but a standard 6201 has a 12 mm bore. The 13 mm bore makes it the non-standard variant, 6201-13. Ordering the standard number off the envelope alone would put a bearing on the shaft that doesn’t fit it — the bore is the dimension that separates the two.

How to identify oneMeasure all three dimensions — bore, outside diameter, width — and treat any one that breaks the standard pattern as a variant to confirm, not a rounding error.

Bearings

Mounted bearing units

A mounted unit combines a bearing insert with a housing — pillow block, flange or take-up — so the shaft support bolts straight down. Identification needs the shaft size, the housing style and bolt pattern, and the locking method: setscrew, eccentric collar or adapter sleeve.

Questions to askWhat shaft size, and which housing style and footprint? How does the insert lock to the shaft? What sealing does the environment need?

Power Transmission

Couplings — identifying what you have

For a coupling: the type, both shaft bores and keyways, and the torque it carries. Flexible couplings — including the CENTAFLEX range — absorb misalignment and shock through an elastic element; rigid and metallic types don’t, so the type has to match the drive, not just the shaft sizes.

Common applicationsCouplings and drives for pumps, conveyors, mixers, fans and packaging lines.

Questions to askWhat is it driving, and at what power and speed? How much misalignment is in the setup? Is the flexible element or spider needed as well — or just the hubs?

Power Transmission

Idler sprockets vs. finished-bore drive sprockets

Both run on roller chain of the same pitch, but they do opposite jobs. A drive sprocket has a finished bore with a keyway — usually with set screws — so it locks to the shaft and transmits torque. An idler sprocket has a bearing pressed into its bore: it spins freely on a fixed shaft or stud and only guides or tensions the chain. It transmits no torque.

To tell them apart, look at the bore. A keyway and set screws mean drive. A smooth bore with a visible bearing — inner race, seals or shields — means idler. For an idler, the dimension that matters is the bearing’s bore, which is the shaft or stud it rides on; for a drive sprocket it’s the finished bore and keyway size. Ordering one in place of the other leaves a sprocket that either free-wheels on a drive shaft or can’t spin on its stud.

How to identify oneCheck the bore first: keyway and set screws = drive sprocket; pressed-in bearing = idler. Then confirm the chain pitch and tooth count, which both types share.

Power Transmission

Belts and chain — reading the markings

For belts, the profile and length markings printed on the belt identify it — the section and length of a V-belt, or the pitch and width of a synchronous (timing) belt. For roller chain, measure the pitch (pin-to-pin distance) and count the strands — single, duplex or triplex — and note whether it is ANSI or ISO/BS standard, as the two are not interchangeable.

Questions to askBelt: which profile, and what length? Chain: what pitch, how many strands, ANSI or ISO/BS?

Seals

Rotary shaft seals, hydraulic seals and O-rings

Seals keep lubricant in and contamination out of rotating and reciprocating equipment. The range covers rotary shaft (oil) seals, hydraulic rod and piston seals, wipers, O-rings, and complete seal kits — including aftermarket kits matched by equipment model.

Common applicationsSeal kits for hydraulic cylinders and gearboxes, shaft seals on pumps and motors, sealing for washdown and process equipment.

Questions to askWhat fluid is it sealing, and at what temperature and pressure? Is it a rod, piston, wiper or rotary application? Full kit, or individual seals?

Seals

Identifying a seal — lip form matters as much as dimensions

Start with the three dimensions — shaft diameter, housing bore and seal width — then look at the lip. Lip form decides what the seal actually does: a single lip with a garter spring retains oil or grease on a rotating shaft, an added dust lip keeps contamination out, and other lip arrangements handle different exclusion and pressure duties. Two seals with identical dimensions and different lip forms are not interchangeable.

Material matters the same way: nitrile (NBR) covers most oil and grease duty, while FKM (fluoroelastomer) suits higher temperatures and more aggressive fluids. A seal isn’t identified until the profile or cross-section, the lip arrangement, the presence of a garter spring, and the material are all confirmed alongside the dimensions.

How to identify oneMeasure shaft, bore and width — then record the lip form (single, double, dust lip), garter spring or not, and the material before treating it as a match.

Seals

Seal kits — the equipment-model route

On hydraulic cylinders, identifying seals one by one isn’t always necessary: seal kits are catalogued by equipment model, so the machine and cylinder model often point straight to the correct kit — rod, piston and wiper seals and wear rings together. When there’s no model to work from, each seal is measured and its profile identified individually.

Questions to askFull kit, or individual seals? If it’s a cylinder application, what are the equipment make and model?

Tube and pipe fittings — thread type first

Thread type and size come first — NPT, BSP and metric threads are not interchangeable, and forcing a mismatch damages both sides of the connection. Then confirm the tube or hose size, the material, the configuration (straight, elbow, tee) and the pressure rating.

Questions to askWhat does it connect to on each end? What pressure does the line see? Stainless, steel or brass — and what’s running through it?

Lockout/tagout devices isolate equipment from its energy sources while maintenance is done. Match the device to the source it isolates — breaker, valve, plug or switch — and make sure every energy source on the machine is accounted for, not just the electrical supply.

Common applicationsLockout/tagout for maintenance and energy isolation across plant equipment.

Questions to askWhat equipment is being locked out, and how many energy sources does it have?

Pumps & Valves

Pumps — confirming a replacement

A pump replacement is confirmed on more than its connections: the pump type (gear, vane or piston in hydraulic service; centrifugal in process service), displacement or flow, operating pressure, shaft rotation, mounting pattern, and port sizes and locations. A pump that bolts up but turns the wrong way, or displaces the wrong volume, is not a match.

Questions to askWhat pressure and flow does the system run at? Which way does the shaft turn? Any nameplate data — make, model, displacement?

Pumps & Valves

Valves — function, spool and porting

Valves direct, limit or regulate flow. Identify by function first — directional, pressure or flow control — then the spool or porting pattern, how the valve is actuated (solenoid, manual or pilot), port sizes and thread type, and the pressure rating. On a directional valve, the spool configuration has to match the original, not just the body size.

Questions to askWhat does the valve control, and how is it actuated? What are the port sizes, and what pressure does the system run at?
